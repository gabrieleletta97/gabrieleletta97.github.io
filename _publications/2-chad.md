The relationship between climatic shocks and conflict remains fiercely debated. While previous research has studied weather shocks during agriculturally relevant periods, it has paid less attention to how their effects vary with proximity to water-dependent livelihoods. Using a panel of finely disaggregated grid cells across Cameroon, Chad, Niger, and Nigeria, we examine how hydroclimatic conditions during locally defined crop-growing seasons predict conflict, with particular attention to Boko Haram violence around Lake Chad. The relationship changes sharply across space. In the full sample, wetter crop seasons are followed by more conflict. Around Lake Chad, however, this pattern reverses: drier crop seasons predict greater subsequent conflict, especially Boko Haram activity, with the strongest associations emerging after two years. Within this area, the negative relationship weakens with distance from surface-water bodies. By linking crop-season shocks to the spatial distribution of water resources, our findings uncover an important source of heterogeneity in the climate–conflict relationship and help reconcile conflicting evidence in the literature.
