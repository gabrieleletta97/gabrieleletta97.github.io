Hazardous alcohol consumption is a major public health concern, yet relatively little is known about how short-run environmental conditions shape drinking behaviour. We estimate the associations of short-run and persistent weather anomalies with drinking participation and hazardous consumption in the UK. We link six years of seven-day drinking diaries from the Alcovision survey to local meteorological observations and measure temperature anomalies relative to station-specific historical conditions and rainfall shocks using upper-tail precipitation thresholds. Weather anomalies show little consistent association with drinking participation, but persistent cold anomalies are associated with higher odds of binge, heavy-binge, and extreme-binge drinking. In the baseline specification, a 30-day cold anomaly, measured as a shock belonging to the lowest quintile of the anomaly distribution, is associated with approximately 5--9\% higher odds of binge, heavy-binge, and extreme-binge drinking. Warm anomalies are associated with lower binge-drinking odds in the upper tails of the distribution. Persistent more-severe rainfall is likewise associated with hazardous drinking. The largest seasonal cold-shock estimates for heavy- and extreme-binge drinking occur in winter. Companion and location estimates suggest that these associations vary with where and with whom drinking occurs.
